Week 7 · Reflective Pause

What I did in the workshop

This week, I returned to Sumpter's experiment and thought about what the process of categorising my friends' posts could actually tell me. Last week, I focused on deciding where each post belonged. Looking back, I became more aware of how much those decisions depended on my own interpretation.

I noticed that my friends rarely shared posts about work or family. However, this does not necessarily mean that these parts of their lives are unimportant to them. They may prefer to keep them private or share them with a different audience. My results showed what was visible to me on one platform, rather than everything that mattered to each person.

Some friends also had fewer than fifteen posts available. This made comparison difficult because the amount of data was different for each person. Even when two people had fifteen posts, those posts could cover very different periods of time. I realised that using the same number of posts did not necessarily make the samples equivalent.

My reflection

Another difficulty was assigning each post to only one category. A photograph of friends at a concert could belong to both “friends” and “music”. Choosing one label meant losing part of its meaning. The chart looked clear, but it did not show the uncertainty behind my choices. Someone else could categorise the same posts differently and produce a different picture of the same person.

This helped me think about the idea of algorithmic identity discussed in class. My table was a small, manual exercise, whereas platforms use much more data and different methods. Still, it helped me understand how categories can turn selected actions into an apparently clear description of a person. When a platform uses that description to select content or advertising, the interpretation can also affect what that person encounters next.

If I repeated the activity, I would use a defined time period and record how many posts were available for each friend. I would also keep notes about posts that were difficult to classify. Asking friends how they interpreted their own posts could help me question my assumptions, although their answers would offer another perspective rather than a single correct interpretation.

For Assessment 1, I want to move beyond describing the steps I followed. One useful starting point is the moment when I struggled to choose a category: what made the decision difficult, what I left out, and how that choice affected the result. This gives me something specific to reflect on instead of simply saying that data cannot fully represent a person.
